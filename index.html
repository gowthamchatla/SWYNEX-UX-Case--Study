QuickTask: a daily task and habit tracker that stays out of the way

How I went from a research problem to a clickable, high-fidelity prototype for people who just want to check things off.

01 · Problem

Tracking small things shouldn’t take a system

Most task and habit apps are built for power users, with projects, tags and analytics. Someone who only wants to track a handful of daily to-dos and habits ends up juggling two or three apps, or giving up within the first week.

Students and early-career professionals need a fast, low-friction way to track daily tasks and simple habits in one place.
GOAL

Add an item in under 10 seconds with no setup.

GOAL

One list for one-off tasks and recurring habits.

GOAL

Progress that encourages instead of shaming.

02 · Research

Who it’s for and what gets in their way

I started with desk research on the two groups most likely to need a lightweight tracker: students (16–24) and early-career professionals (22–30). The personas below are proto-personas built from that research; validating them with interviews is the next step.

Studious Sam, 20

University student. Wants to keep up with assignments and study habits. Finds Notion too heavy for a simple to-do. “I just want to check things off.”

Busy Priya, 26

Junior software engineer. Wants personal habits kept apart from work tasks. Dislikes guilt-driven streak warnings and extra subscriptions.

03 · Structure and flow

A small structure with one important flow

The app has a one-time onboarding screen and three tabs: Home, Progress and Profile. Adding an item is a focused form opened from Home, not a tab, so people return straight to what they were doing.

The flow I focused on is creating an item, since research pointed to friction there. Validation sits inside the flow: an invalid name shows an inline error and the user fixes it without leaving the form.

04 · Wireframes

Structure first, polish later

Low-fidelity grayscale wireframes kept feedback on layout and hierarchy rather than colour. The main decisions here: a summary at the top of Home, one combined list, a thumb-reachable add button and a shallow three-tab bar.

05 · Final interface

Calm, clear and consistent

The visual language is deliberately quiet: one blue accent, generous spacing and a single typeface (Inter), so the user’s own tasks are the loudest thing on screen. Everything is built from a small set of tokens, which keeps screens consistent.

Colour

Accent #378ADD, dark accent #185FA5, error #D85A30 and neutral surfaces.

Type

Inter at 24, 16, 14, 12 and 11 pt, with weights 400 to 700.

Spacing

4-point scale (4, 8, 12, 16, 24) with radius 12 for inputs and 14 for buttons.

06 · Prototype

Try it yourself

All five screens are connected in an interactive prototype with a working add flow, inline validation, checking items off and a dark mode toggle. A coded version of the app also runs on plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with data saved in the browser.

  • Tap Get started, then tap a task to mark it done.
  • Press +, try saving an empty name, then a valid one.
  • Use the bottom tabs for Progress and Profile, then switch on dark mode.

07 · Key design decisions

What I decided, and why

Feedback

Summary on top of Home

Shows “3 of 5 done” and the streak at a glance, with no red overdue warnings.

Simplicity

One combined list

Tasks and habits live on one screen in two labelled groups, so nothing is split across apps.

Speed

Name is the only required field

Type, category and reminder have defaults, so adding takes well under 10 seconds.

Ergonomics

Floating add button

Bottom right, always visible and reachable with the thumb.

Trust

Inline validation

Errors appear beside the field, not in a pop-up, so users fix them in context.

Navigation

Three tabs only

Any screen is at most two taps away, with no hidden menus.

08 · Learnings and next steps

What I’d do next

  • Validate with real users. The personas come from desk research, so I’d run short interviews and time how long a first item takes to save.
  • Add accessibility checks. Test colour contrast and tap target sizes, especially the accent colour in dark mode.
  • Review the Progress screen. Real usage would show which stats people look at.

Biggest lesson: constraints made the design better. Keeping a single required field and three tabs forced every other decision to earn its place.
